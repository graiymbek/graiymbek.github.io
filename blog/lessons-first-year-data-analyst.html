5 Things I Wish I Knew Starting Out as a Data Analyst

From trusting a dataset too quickly to learning that a dashboard nobody opens isn't a finished project: lessons from my first year doing this for real.

October 7, 20265 min read

Every one of these lessons cost me something: a little rework, a slightly awkward meeting, or just time I didn't need to spend. None of them are complicated. All of them are things I only really believed once I'd lived through them once.

1. Trusting a dataset too quickly is the most expensive mistake you can make

Coming from research, I thought I already had this instinct, and I mostly did, but production business data breaks in different ways than lab data. A "customer ID" field with duplicates that aren't really duplicates. A date column that's actually three different date grains depending on which system wrote the row. The habit that's saved me the most time is boring and non-negotiable: profile the data before you model it. Row counts, null rates, and duplicate checks on your grain, every time, before a single DAX measure gets written.

2. A dashboard nobody opens isn't a finished project

I used to consider a dashboard "done" when it was technically correct and visually clean. I don't anymore. If the person it was built for doesn't open it after week two, something upstream went wrong: wrong grain, wrong cadence, or it just answers a question nobody actually had. Now I ask what decision this dashboard is supposed to change before I build anything, and I check back in with whoever asked for it before I consider it shipped, not just when I hand it off.

3. SQL fundamentals matter more than the flashiest DAX trick

It's tempting to chase the clever DAX pattern: the one that collapses five measures into one elegant one. What's actually paid off more consistently is being rock-solid on SQL: joins, window functions, CTEs, and knowing when to push logic upstream into the data model instead of downstream into a measure. A clean, well-indexed query that returns the right grain saves more headaches than any DAX trick saves time.

4. Document your data model like someone else has to maintain it, because eventually, they do

Early on I kept model logic in my head: why this relationship is one-to-many, why that measure uses CALCULATE with a specific filter removed, why there's a bridge table instead of a direct relationship. The first time I had to hand a model off, or came back to my own work after three months on something else, I understood why that's a bad habit. A short README or even inline descriptions on measures pays for itself the very first time someone besides you opens the file.

5. Ask "what decision will this enable" before you open Power BI

This is the one I'd tell myself on day one if I could. It's easy to jump straight to building because building is the fun part. But the dashboards that actually got used were the ones where I'd pinned down, in one sentence, what decision someone would make differently because this existed. If I couldn't answer that before starting, the project usually needed more conversation, not more charts.

None of these are things you can shortcut by reading about them. I certainly didn't, which is a little bit the point. But if even one of them saves you a round of rework on your first real project, that's the post doing its job.
